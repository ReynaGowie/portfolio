Students spend years experiencing course design from the receiving end. We notice when instructions confuse us, when discussions work, and when an assignment asks for more than it acknowledges.

That experience should count when universities decide how learning happens. Creating my own philosophy course has also taught me how much responsibility comes with having a say in those decisions.

## Give students a role before the evaluation

A recent [ASU News article about Project Gen Z](https://news.asu.edu/20260223-science-and-technology-project-gen-z-asks-whether-college-teaching-working-current) describes undergraduates helping researchers investigate student learning. They help shape questions, interpret findings, and recommend changes to a biology course.

What interests me is where students enter the process. They’re helping decide what needs investigating, with opportunities to influence the course beyond commenting on decisions that have already been made.

As president of Penn State’s Students Teaching Students, I support undergraduates developing real, for-credit courses with faculty supervision. My own course, *Guess Who? Identity, Recognition, and Representation*, launches in Spring 2027.

## Having a say means taking responsibility

For the final project, students will represent a classmate through an identity card and presentation. That sounds fun. It also means someone else gets to tell your story.

My hardest design decision was how much structure to put around privacy and consent. Too little could leave students exposed; too much could make an open discussion seminar feel procedural.

On Professor John Christman’s suggestion, I added a two-week foundations unit before partner work. I also wrote guidelines covering privacy, consent, the right to withhold information, and respectful representation.

Students will have the right to correct their representation and access an alternative assignment. I also divided the final project into six graded checkpoints, creating opportunities to review work before the final presentation.

The course hasn’t run yet, so I can’t claim these choices have worked. Their purpose is to let students explore identity while keeping control over what they share.

Designing the course made me responsible for questions I might otherwise have encountered only as a student. Universities should give more students that opportunity, with guidance and room to revise.

We already experience the design. We should help shape it.
